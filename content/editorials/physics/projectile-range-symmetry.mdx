## The problem

Throw a ball from level ground at a fixed speed. Could two different
launch angles put it at the same landing point? Make a prediction
before opening the slider. If they can, would the flights also reach
the same maximum height and last the same amount of time, or would
those parts of the motion differ? Then compare launches at $30°$ and
$60°$ while keeping the speed at $20\,\mathrm{m/s}$. Assume flat ground,
the same launch and landing height, and no air resistance.

## The idea

Start with what changes as you tilt the launch. A shallow throw sends
the ball forward quickly but keeps it in the air for a short time. A
steep throw gives it less forward speed but keeps it in the air longer.
Range is forward speed multiplied by time in the air. For two
complementary launch angles (angles that add to 90 degrees), the increase
in time balances the decrease in forward speed exactly, so the horizontal
distances match.

Equal range does not mean equal flight. The steeper throw reaches a
higher point and stays in the air longer, even though it lands at the
same distance. This is why the path matters when a problem asks about
height or flight time, not only the landing point.

Drag the angle slider below $45°$ and above it. The range markers line
up for complementary pairs, but the *shape* of the flight differs. A
low, flat arc and a high, steep one reach the same point by very
different paths.

## Full proof

**Setup.** Launch speed $v_0$, launch angle $\theta$, no air resistance,
flat ground. Here $g$ is the magnitude of gravitational acceleration.
The horizontal position $x$ and the height $y$ at time $t$ are:

$$x(t) = v_0 \cos\theta \cdot t, \qquad y(t) = v_0 \sin\theta \cdot t - \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2$$

**Time of flight.** The projectile lands when $y(t) = 0$ for $t > 0$
(the solution $t = 0$ is the launch itself):

$$t = 0 \quad\text{or}\quad t = \frac{2 v_0 \sin\theta}{g}$$

so the nonzero flight time is $T = \dfrac{2 v_0 \sin\theta}{g}$.

**Range.** Substituting $T$ into $x(t)$:

$$R(\theta) = v_0 \cos\theta \cdot T = \frac{2 v_0^2 \sin\theta\cos\theta}{g} = \frac{v_0^2 \sin(2\theta)}{g}$$

using the double-angle identity $2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \sin(2\theta)$.

**Symmetry.** Compare $R(\theta)$ and $R(90° - \theta)$:

$$R(90° - \theta) = \frac{v_0^2 \sin\big(2(90° - \theta)\big)}{g} = \frac{v_0^2 \sin(180° - 2\theta)}{g}$$

and since $\sin(180° - x) = \sin(x)$ for any $x$:

$$\sin(180° - 2\theta) = \sin(2\theta) \implies R(90° - \theta) = R(\theta) \qquad \blacksquare$$

**Where it breaks in practice.** The error this editorial is filed
under, treating equal range as equal trajectory, shows up whenever a
problem asks about the *flight itself* (maximum height, flight time,
vertical impact speed) and not just where it lands. Those quantities are
not symmetric under $\theta \leftrightarrow 90° - \theta$: maximum
height scales with $\sin^2\theta$, which differs for complementary
angles unless $\theta = 45°$. The sandbox above shows the whole arc, not
just the endpoint, so you can see it.
