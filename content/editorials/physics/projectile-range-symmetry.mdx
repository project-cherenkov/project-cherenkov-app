## The problem

Launch a projectile at $30°$ and it lands some distance away. Launch it
again at the same speed but at $60°$, and it lands in *exactly the same
spot*. The same thing happens for any angle $\theta$: launching at
$\theta$ and at $90° - \theta$ gives the same range, the horizontal
distance travelled. (Here we assume flat ground and no air resistance.)
Why?

## The idea

Start with what changes as you tilt the launch. A shallow launch sends
the projectile forward quickly but keeps it in the air only briefly. A
steep launch keeps it in the air for a long time but gives it little
forward speed. Range is forward speed times time in the air, and for
angles that add up to $90°$ these two effects trade off exactly.

The maths shows why. Range depends on the launch angle only through
$\sin(2\theta)$, and the sine function has a symmetry:
$\sin(2\theta) = \sin(180° - 2\theta) = \sin\big(2(90° - \theta)\big)$.
That symmetry does the work. Two angles that add up to $90°$ are called
complementary. For the complementary pair $\theta$ and $90° - \theta$,
the values inside the sine are $2\theta$ and $180° - 2\theta$. These are
mirror images across $90°$, and the sine gives the same result for both.

Drag the angle slider below $45°$ and above it. The range markers line

low, flat arc and a high, steep one reach the same point by very
different paths.

## Full proof

**Setup.** Launch speed $v_0$, launch angle $\theta$, no air resistance,
flat ground. The horizontal position $x$ and the height $y$ at time $t$
are:

$$x(t) = v_0 \cos\theta \cdot t, \qquad y(t) = v_0 \sin\theta \cdot t - \tfrac{1}{2} g t^2$$

**Time of flight.** The projectile lands when $y(t) = 0$ for $t > 0$
(the solution $t = 0$ is the launch itself):

$$t = 0 \quad\text{or}\quad t = \frac{2 v_0 \sin\theta}{g}$$

so the nonzero flight time is $T = \dfrac{2 v_0 \sin\theta}{g}$.

**Range.** Substituting $T$ into $x(t)$:

$$R(\theta) = v_0 \cos\theta \cdot T = \frac{2 v_0^2 \sin\theta\cos\theta}{g} = \frac{v_0^2 \sin(2\theta)}{g}$$

using the double-angle identity $2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \sin(2\theta)$.

**Symmetry.** Compare $R(\theta)$ and $R(90° - \theta)$:

$$R(90° - \theta) = \frac{v_0^2 \sin\big(2(90° - \theta)\big)}{g} = \frac{v_0^2 \sin(180° - 2\theta)}{g}$$

and since $\sin(180° - x) = \sin(x)$ for any $x$:

$$\sin(180° - 2\theta) = \sin(2\theta) \implies R(90° - \theta) = R(\theta) \qquad \blacksquare$$

**Where it breaks in practice.** The error this editorial is filed
under, treating equal range as equal trajectory, shows up whenever a
problem asks about the *flight itself* (maximum height, flight time,
vertical impact speed) and not just where it lands. Those quantities are
not symmetric under $\theta \leftrightarrow 90° - \theta$: maximum
height scales with $\sin^2\theta$, which differs for complementary
angles unless $\theta = 45°$. The sandbox above shows the whole arc, not
just the endpoint, so you can see it.
