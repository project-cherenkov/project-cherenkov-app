## The problem

Two planets orbit the same star. They have the same orbital period and
the same planet-to-star size ratio. One orbit is a circle. The other is
a stretched oval, which astronomers call an *eccentric* orbit (the more
stretched, the higher the eccentricity). Do their transits last the same
amount of time? A transit is the dip in starlight while the planet
crosses the star's disc.

It's tempting to say yes, because "same period, same sizes" sounds like
it should settle everything. In fact the durations differ, and the
reason is the planet's speed.

## The idea

A planet on an eccentric orbit does not move at a constant speed. It
moves fastest at periapsis (its closest approach to the star) and
slowest at apoapsis (its farthest point). That is Kepler's second law:
the line from the star to the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal
times.

Think of a car crossing a bridge of fixed length. The faster the car,
the shorter the crossing. The star's disc is the bridge. If the transit
happens near periapsis, the planet is moving quickly and crosses
quickly. Near apoapsis it moves slowly, so the same crossing takes
longer.

## Full proof

**Setup.** By definition, the mean anomaly $M$ increases uniformly in
time: $M = nt$. Here $n = 2\pi/T$ is the mean motion and $T$ is the
orbital period. Think of $M$ as the hand of a steady clock, while the
planet's real position and speed vary. Kepler's equation relates $M$ to
the eccentric anomaly $E$, an angle that locates the planet on its
orbit:

$$M = E - e\sin E \implies \frac{dM}{dE} = 1 - e\cos E$$

**Modelling convention.** We treat a transit as the planet crossing a
fixed window of eccentric anomaly $[-\Delta E, \Delta E]$, centred on
periapsis ($E = 0$). This is a simplification. A real transit is a fixed
window of *true* anomaly, which is set by the star's actual angular size
as seen from the planet. The two agree only to leading order for small
$\Delta E$, and a distant star's disc puts us in that regime anyway.
This is exactly the convention the interactive above computes.

**Duration.** Because $M$ is uniform in time, the time spent between
$E=-\Delta E$ and $E=\Delta E$ is:

$$\Delta t = \frac{\Delta M}{n} = \frac{1}{n}\int_{-\Delta E}^{\Delta E} (1 - e\cos E)\, dE$$

For small $\Delta E$, $\cos E \approx 1$ across the whole window, which
gives:

$$\Delta t \approx \frac{2\Delta E(1-e)}{n} = \frac{\Delta E (1-e)\, T}{\pi} \qquad \blacksquare$$

**Consequence.** For a transit lined up with periapsis, the duration
shrinks linearly toward zero as $e \to 1$, because the planet is moving
fastest exactly where the transit happens. Flip the alignment to
apoapsis and the effect reverses: eccentricity would *lengthen* the
transit instead.

**Where it breaks in practice.** The error this editorial is filed
under, assuming constant orbital speed, is really two errors that
compound. First, forgetting that speed varies around an eccentric orbit
at all. Second, even granting that, assuming the *transit* necessarily
lands at periapsis. Real transiting systems can have any argument of
periapsis (an angle that fixes where periapsis lies relative to our line
of sight), and duration depends on both. This sandbox fixes the
alignment to periapsis to isolate the eccentricity effect. Don't reach
for the formula above on a system where periapsis alignment isn't given.
