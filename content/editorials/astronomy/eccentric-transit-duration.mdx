## The problem

Imagine comparing two planets around the same star. They have the same
orbital period and the same planet-to-star size ratio. Both cross the
star's disk centrally, so their transit chords are alike. One follows a
circular orbit; the other follows an *eccentric* orbit and crosses in
front of the star near periapsis, its closest point to the star. Would
their transits last the same amount of time? Make a prediction before
reading on. A transit is the dip in starlight while a planet crosses
the star's disk.

## The idea

A planet on an eccentric orbit does not move at a constant speed. It
moves fastest at periapsis (its closest approach to the star) and
slowest at apoapsis (its farthest point). That is Kepler's second law:
the line from the star to the planet sweeps out equal areas in equal
times.

Think of a car crossing a bridge of fixed length. The faster the car,
the shorter the crossing. The star's disk is the bridge. If the transit
happens near periapsis, the planet is moving quickly and crosses
quickly. Near apoapsis it moves slowly, so the same crossing takes
longer.

The sandbox below covers the simplest case, a transit lined up with
periapsis. A single eccentricity value sets both the shape of the orbit
and the light curve (the graph of brightness over time) underneath it.
Push eccentricity up and the dip gets narrower. The model isolates
this alignment rather than covering every possible transit geometry.

## Full proof

**Setup.** Let $e$ denote orbital eccentricity. By definition, the
mean anomaly $M$ increases uniformly in time: $M = nt$. Here
$n = 2\pi/T$ is the mean motion and $T$ is the orbital period. Think of $M$ as the hand of a steady clock, while the
planet's real position and speed vary. Kepler's equation relates $M$ to
the eccentric anomaly $E$, an angle that locates the planet on its
orbit:

$$M = E - e\sin E \implies \frac{dM}{dE} = 1 - e\cos E$$

**Modeling convention.** We treat a transit as the planet crossing a
fixed window of eccentric anomaly $[-\Delta E, \Delta E]$, centered on
periapsis ($E = 0$). This is a simplification chosen to make the effect
of orbital speed visible. A real small-star, central transit is better
approximated by a fixed physical chord across the star's disk. Its
angular window changes with the planet's distance from the star. The
interactive above uses the fixed eccentric-anomaly window, not that
fixed-chord model.

**Duration.** Because $M$ is uniform in time, the time spent between
$E=-\Delta E$ and $E=\Delta E$ is:

$$\Delta t = \frac{\Delta M}{n} = \frac{1}{n}\int_{-\Delta E}^{\Delta E} (1 - e\cos E)\, dE$$

For small $\Delta E$, $\cos E \approx 1$ across the whole window, which
gives:

$$\Delta t \approx \frac{2\Delta E(1-e)}{n} = \frac{\Delta E (1-e)\, T}{\pi} \qquad \blacksquare$$

**Scope of the linear factor.** The factor $1-e$ belongs to this
fixed-eccentric-anomaly convention. It is not the usual scaling for a
real small-star, central transit. For a fixed physical chord at
periapsis, let $D_p$ be the transit duration and $D_c$ the duration
for a circular orbit with the same semi-major axis and chord. Their
ratio in this approximation is

$$
\frac{D_p}{D_c} = \sqrt{\frac{1-e}{1+e}}.
$$

At $e=0.8$, this model gives $0.20$, while the fixed-chord approximation
gives $0.33$. Both predict a shorter periapsis transit as eccentricity
increases, but their functional forms differ. The model's linear
factor must not be read as a general transit-duration law.

**Consequence.** Near periapsis, the planet moves faster and crosses a
given chord sooner. Near apoapsis, it moves more slowly, so the transit
lasts longer under the corresponding fixed-chord approximation.

**Where it breaks in practice.** The error this editorial is filed
under, assuming constant orbital speed, is really two errors that
compound. First, forgetting that speed varies around an eccentric orbit
at all. Second, even granting that, assuming the *transit* necessarily
lands at periapsis. Real transiting systems can have any argument of
periapsis (an angle that fixes where periapsis lies relative to our line
of sight), and duration depends on both. This sandbox fixes the
alignment to periapsis to isolate the eccentricity effect. Do not apply
these periapsis-aligned factors when the system's argument of periapsis
is unknown.
