## The problem

Imagine a closed box, called a cavity, whose walls are held at a fixed temperature. After a while the walls and the radiation inside settle at the same temperature, which is called thermal equilibrium. The cavity then holds electromagnetic radiation spread continuously over all wavelengths. Classical physics (physics before quantum theory) gives a formula for how much radiation appears at each wavelength. That amount is called the spectral radiance. Follow the formula toward shorter and shorter wavelengths and the spectral radiance keeps increasing. It has no upper limit as the wavelength approaches zero.

That can't be right. A body at a finite temperature should not emit an infinite amount of energy. So why does the classical formula predict it, and what does the real spectrum do instead?

## The idea

The formula is the Rayleigh–Jeans law. It is an approximation, and it works when the wavelength is long compared with a characteristic thermal wavelength, a length scale set by the temperature. The mistake is to apply it at every wavelength.

In the classical picture, the radiation in the cavity splits into *modes*, the separate wave patterns that fit inside it. Each mode receives an average energy of about $kT$, where $k$ is the Boltzmann constant and $T$ is temperature. Higher frequencies fit more modes into the cavity. Classical theory therefore predicts more and more radiation as frequency rises, and it has nothing that holds the very high frequencies back.

Planck's law changes the result. It treats energy exchange as happening in discrete packets, called quanta, each with energy $h\nu$, where $h$ is the Planck constant and $\nu$ is frequency. A high-frequency quantum carries more energy, so it is less likely to be thermally excited: the heat energy available, about $kT$, is rarely enough to produce one. The spectrum therefore falls at short wavelengths instead of increasing without limit.

## Full argument

The plan has four steps. Start from Planck's law. Take its long-wavelength limit to get the Rayleigh–Jeans law. Show that the Rayleigh–Jeans law gives an infinite total. Then check that Planck's law does not.

A *blackbody* is an ideal object that absorbs all incident radiation and emits radiation determined only by its temperature. Let $T$ be its absolute temperature in kelvin, and let $\lambda$ be wavelength in metres. The spectral radiance per unit wavelength, $B_\lambda$, measures radiated power per unit area, per unit wavelength interval, and per unit solid angle. (A solid angle measures how wide a cone of directions is.) Its SI unit is $\mathrm{W\,m^{-3}\,sr^{-1}}$.

Planck's law gives the spectrum:

$$
B_\lambda(T)=\frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\frac{1}{e^{hc/(\lambda kT)}-1}.
$$

Here $h$ is the Planck constant, $c$ is the speed of light in vacuum, and $k$ is the Boltzmann constant. Define the dimensionless quantity (a pure number with no units)

$$
x=\frac{hc}{\lambda kT}.
$$

At long wavelengths, $x$ is much smaller than one. The exponential can then be approximated by its first two terms: $e^x\approx 1+x$. Therefore, $e^x-1\approx x$. Substituting this approximation into Planck's law gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
B_\lambda(T)&\approx \frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\frac{1}{x}\\
&=\frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}\frac{\lambda kT}{hc}\\
&=\frac{2ckT}{\lambda^4}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the Rayleigh–Jeans law in wavelength form. The derivation also shows when it holds: it requires $hc/(\lambda kT)\ll 1$, so it describes only the long-wavelength end of the spectrum.

The ultraviolet catastrophe appears when this limit is ignored. The total radiance is obtained by adding up (integrating) the spectral radiance over all wavelengths. If the Rayleigh–Jeans expression were valid everywhere, its wavelength dependence would make the integral proportional to

$$
\int_0^\infty \lambda^{-4}\,d\lambda.
$$

Introduce a positive lower wavelength cutoff $\epsilon$, the smallest wavelength we agree to include. The integral from $\epsilon$ to infinity is

$$
\int_\epsilon^\infty \lambda^{-4}\,d\lambda
=\left[-\frac{1}{3\lambda^3}\right]_\epsilon^\infty
=\frac{1}{3\epsilon^3}.
$$

As $\epsilon$ approaches zero, this expression grows without limit. So the classical extrapolation predicts an infinite total radiance. The error is using the approximation at short wavelengths, where its condition $hc/(\lambda kT)\ll 1$ is false.

Planck's law avoids this divergence. At short wavelengths, $x$ is much larger than one, so $e^x-1\approx e^x$. The spectrum then behaves as

$$
B_\lambda(T)\approx \frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5}e^{-hc/(\lambda kT)}.
$$

The exponential suppression dominates the factor $\lambda^{-5}$ as $\lambda$ approaches zero, so the spectral radiance tends to zero. At long wavelengths, Planck's law approaches the Rayleigh–Jeans expression, whose $\lambda^{-4}$ tail dies away fast enough that the area under it out to infinity is finite. The Planck spectrum therefore has a finite integral over all wavelengths.

The physical change is energy quantization. Planck's model allows energy to be exchanged only in quanta of size $h\nu$, with $\nu=c/\lambda$. In thermal equilibrium, the mean thermal energy of a quantized oscillator mode, above its zero-point energy (the minimum energy a quantum oscillator always keeps), is $h\nu/[e^{h\nu/(kT)}-1]$. When $h\nu$ is much larger than the thermal energy scale $kT$, the mode is hardly ever excited, because its thermal occupation is exponentially suppressed. Classical equipartition instead gives every mode the same average energy $kT$, whatever its frequency. That assumption is the source of the high-frequency excess.

The integrated Planck spectrum is finite and proportional to the fourth power of temperature. In the notation of *Fundamental Astronomy*, the total intensity is $B(T)=AT^4$, where $A=2\pi^4k^4/(15c^2h^3)$. For isotropic blackbody radiation (the same in every direction), the emitted flux is $F=\pi B(T)=\sigma T^4$, where $\sigma$ is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant. This is the Stefan–Boltzmann law.

**Where the argument applies.** The derivation assumes an ideal blackbody in thermal equilibrium. Real stars and other hot objects are not perfect blackbodies, although their continuous spectra can often be approximated by blackbody spectra. The visualization uses the ideal spectrum and a finite wavelength interval. It shows the divergence through the rising Rayleigh–Jeans curve, but it cannot draw the zero-wavelength limit itself.
