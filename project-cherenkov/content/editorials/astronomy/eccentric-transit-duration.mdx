## The problem

Two planets, same orbital period, same star, same planet-to-star size
ratio — one on a circular orbit, one on an eccentric one. Do their transits
(the dip in starlight while the planet crosses the star's disc) last the
same amount of time? It's tempting to say yes, since "same period, same
sizes" sounds like it should fix everything else. It doesn't.

## The idea

A planet on an eccentric orbit does **not** move at constant angular speed
— it moves fastest near periapsis (closest approach) and slowest near
apoapsis (farthest point). That's Kepler's second law: equal areas swept in
equal times. If a transit happens to occur near periapsis, the planet is
whipping through that part of its orbit and crosses the star's disc
quickly. Near apoapsis, it dawdles, and the same disc-crossing takes
longer.

## Full proof

**Setup.** Mean anomaly $M$ increases uniformly in time by definition:
$M = nt$, where $n = 2\pi/T$ is the mean motion and $T$ is the orbital
period. Kepler's equation relates $M$ to the eccentric anomaly $E$:

$$M = E - e\sin E \implies \frac{dM}{dE} = 1 - e\cos E$$

**Modeling convention.** Treat a transit as the planet crossing a fixed
window of eccentric anomaly $[-\Delta E, \Delta E]$ centered on periapsis
($E = 0$) — a simplification. (A real transit is a fixed window of *true*
anomaly — the star's actual angular size as seen from the planet — not
eccentric anomaly; the two only agree to leading order for small $\Delta E$,
which is the regime a distant star's disc puts us in anyway.) This is
exactly the convention the interactive above computes.

**Duration.** Because $M$ is uniform in time, the time spent between
$E=-\Delta E$ and $E=\Delta E$ is:

$$\Delta t = \frac{\Delta M}{n} = \frac{1}{n}\int_{-\Delta E}^{\Delta E} (1 - e\cos E)\, dE$$

For small $\Delta E$, $\cos E \approx 1$ across the whole window, giving:

$$\Delta t \approx \frac{2\Delta E(1-e)}{n} = \frac{\Delta E (1-e)\, T}{\pi} \qquad \blacksquare$$

**Consequence.** For a periapsis-aligned transit, duration shrinks linearly
toward zero as $e \to 1$ — the planet is moving fastest exactly where the
transit happens. Flip the alignment to apoapsis and the sign of the effect
flips too: eccentricity would *lengthen* the transit instead.

**Where it breaks in practice.** The error this editorial is filed under —
assuming constant orbital speed — is really two errors that compound: (1)
forgetting that speed varies around an eccentric orbit at all, and (2)
even granting that, assuming the *transit* necessarily lands at periapsis.
Real transiting systems can have any argument of periapsis, and duration
depends on both — this sandbox fixes the alignment to periapsis to isolate
just the eccentricity effect cleanly. Don't reach for the formula above on
a system where periapsis-alignment isn't actually given.
